# SoccerShooter Stage 05R — Professional Motion, Dribbling, and Contact Overhaul

**Status:** BLOCKING OWNER-FEEL REMEDIATION. The current Stage 05–07 engineering branch is not approved for merge. The 74.87 s owner playtest on 2026-10-18/19 is the acceptance evidence that reopens the Stage 05 feel gate.

**Baseline branch:** `stage/05-07-owner-control-overhaul`  
**Baseline head when this plan was authored:** `8d5f2099b2b87d9f02ad42da3abc848d143a2377`  
**Primary goal:** Make movement and ball control feel like a coordinated professional footballer rather than a point-mass controller repeatedly correcting a free ball.

> **For agentic workers:** REQUIRED SUB-SKILL: use `superpowers:systematic-debugging` for every playtest defect, `superpowers:test-driven-development` for simulation changes, and `superpowers:executing-plans` or `superpowers:subagent-driven-development` when implementing this plan. Do not declare feel complete from automated tests alone.

## Why this remediation exists

The existing implementation is deterministic, bounded, and mechanically safer than the first Stage 05 build, but the owner playtest still reads and feels like a debug physics sandbox. The core problem is architectural rather than another tuning problem.

Today the player is represented authoritatively by position, velocity, facing yaw, stamina, grounded state, and one coarse locomotion label. Dribbling is generated by periodically checking a cooldown and issuing a bounded velocity correction that moves the ball toward an invisible control corridor. Shooting and passing similarly become legal and then apply a solved impulse immediately. This can satisfy distance and determinism tests while still failing the human requirement that every movement and ball contact look and feel caused by a plausible athlete action.

This plan replaces that weak abstraction with a lightweight deterministic **Intent → Steps → Contact** pipeline. The root/player simulation remains authoritative. The ball remains a truly free Stage 04 physical object. The new layer plans the athlete's near-future trajectory, gait/contact opportunity, foot choice, and ball interaction before an impulse may occur.

This is **not** the full Stage 12/13 final-character pipeline and it is **not** a massive motion-matching or runtime-RL project. It pulls forward only the minimum motion/contact semantics required to judge Stage 05 honestly. Final character art, large animation libraries, motion capture cleanup, and premium animation presentation remain Stage 12/13 work.

# 1. Owner-video findings

The 74.87 s playtest was reviewed across the complete recording in dense 0.5 s samples and targeted sections. The recurring problems are systemic rather than one isolated bad maneuver.

## 1.1 0–12 s — control is functional but the athlete is not physically explaining the ball

- The ball can be carried, approached, and redirected, but the visible athlete remains an upright marker with no stride, support foot, body lean, or contact surface.
- The ball changes behavior when the controller allows a touch, not when a visible foot reaches it.
- Close control therefore reads as a hidden force field even though the ball position is never literally welded or teleported.
- Small directional changes have weak visual anticipation. The body can reorient while the ball correction happens independently.

## 1.2 12–20 s — locomotion itself reads as root translation rather than athletic movement

- The player travels and turns with little visual distinction between acceleration, steady gait, braking, planting, and re-acceleration.
- Camera motion exposes the stiffness because there is no lower-body cadence for the eye to anchor to.
- The current root controller may be numerically smooth, but the missing gait state makes it read as sliding/gliding.

## 1.3 20–34 s — dribble direction changes expose the synthetic controller

- The user can turn and recover the ball, but the sequence lacks a believable plant step and foot-specific effort touch.
- The ball can be steered back into a useful lane through corridor correction without a clear physical cause.
- A professional dribbler should visibly shorten or reshape steps before a hard turn, lower/reorient the body, use a viable foot/surface, make the contact, then accelerate out. None of those states currently exist authoritatively.

## 1.4 34–50 s — goal-area handling still feels like independent root + ball systems

- Approaches near the goal show the same generic locomotion grammar as open-field carrying.
- There is no strong gather/setup beat before a football action.
- The camera follows the root competently but does not anticipate the action or frame the relationship between athlete, ball, approach path, and target strongly enough.

## 1.5 50–74.87 s — shots confirm the same contact architecture problem

- The player can approach, aim, charge, and shoot, but there is no support-foot/gather/back-swing/contact/follow-through sequence.
- Once the action is legal, the ball receives the solution. The visible body is not the reason the contact happened.
- The same generic follow-camera language continues through cuts and shots, reducing action readability and perceived weight.

## Owner-level conclusion

The current build is not failing because the ball simulation is obviously bad. Stage 04 ball flight/ground physics is one of the strongest parts of the project. The build is failing because **player movement, contact timing, body mechanics, ball impulse, and presentation are not yet one coherent system**.

# 2. Codebase root-cause audit

## 2.1 `packages/sim/src/player/PlayerState.ts`

Current authoritative movement state is too small to express professional football movement.

Missing concepts include:
- gait/stride phase;
- current support foot;
- next viable contact foot;
- plant state and plant direction;
- braking/propulsion phase;
- center-of-mass lean/turn demand;
- body angular velocity / turn demand;
- short-horizon trajectory samples;
- action preparation/contact/recovery phase;
- intended ball-contact time.

A coarse `sharp-cut` string cannot represent the sequence that makes a sharp cut believable.

## 2.2 `packages/sim/src/player/movement.ts`

The controller is currently a good deterministic kinematic shell, but it is still a point-mass response model:
- camera-relative desired direction;
- target speed;
- heading-rate cap;
- velocity move-toward;
- a hard plant-cut threshold that mainly lowers target speed;
- facing yaw independently moves toward aim yaw.

That is enough for Stage 03 greybox navigation. It is not enough to make a footballer visibly brake into a plant step, transfer weight, and accelerate out.

## 2.3 `packages/sim/src/football/runtime.ts`

The current code explicitly states that control cooldowns are "control-contact opportunities, not literal animation beats." Current control cadence is represented by mode-specific timers around 0.08–0.20 s.

That is the key abstraction to remove. A professional-feeling contact opportunity must come from gait phase + ball trajectory + viable foot geometry, not elapsed cooldown alone.

## 2.4 `packages/sim/src/football/controlEnvelope.ts`

The current `corridorCorrection(...)` is the main synthetic-dribbling mechanism. It:
- projects ball error into forward/lateral components;
- calculates a desired control pocket;
- issues bounded forward/recovery/lateral velocity corrections;
- adds deterministic momentum retention for hard conditions.

It is safer than magnetic possession because it never teleports the ball. But it still behaves like an invisible servo around the player.

The controller has no persistent left/right foot, contact surface, stride timing, or support-foot context. `targetPoint` is planning geometry only; it is not a location a simulated foot actually reaches at a planned time.

## 2.5 `apps/web/src/dev/FootballPractice.ts`

The integration exposes the mismatch directly:
- when `controlTouchCooldown <= 0`, `buildControlTouch(...)` is called;
- if legal, its ball command is applied immediately;
- shot release similarly calls `buildShot(...)` and applies the command immediately;
- pass release does the same.

There is no shared contact scheduler between simulation and presentation.

## 2.6 Current tests over-credit containment

Existing dribble tests prove useful invariants such as:
- no teleport;
- no out-of-reach cascade;
- bounded separation;
- deterministic pressure/error;
- sprint remains recoverable.

They do **not** prove that a plausible foot could make every contact. The current "professional physical ball-control course" can apply a legal touch whenever the cooldown is free, so it can pass while the owner still sees synthetic handling.

The next test suite must make **contact provenance** a first-class invariant.

## 2.7 Camera

`ThirdPersonCamera` is a clean generic spring camera, but it mainly tracks the player root with manual yaw/pitch and sprint FOV. `FootballPracticeCamera` adds limited loose-ball bias and goal collision. It does not yet reason about:
- current velocity and near-future path;
- plant/cut direction;
- ball-contact preparation;
- shot/pass setup;
- keeping player + ball + intended contact point readable during abrupt maneuvers.

This is a presentation problem, not authoritative gameplay, but it materially affects perceived control quality.

# 3. Research basis

This plan uses public production knowledge and peer-reviewed sports/graphics research as design evidence. It does not claim access to proprietary EA implementation details.

## 3.1 EA / production football-game lessons

### FIFA / FC animation warping

EA's GDC session **Animation Warping for Responsiveness in FIFA Soccer** describes procedurally altering captured turns and warping motion to reach a required point at a required time. The important design principle for SoccerShooter is **target-at-time motion correction**, not blindly blending or snapping.

Source: GDC Vault, Paul McComas and Jason Sanmiya, `https://gdcvault.com/play/1012884/Animation-Warping-for-Responsiveness-in`

### HyperMotion / ML-Flow / Technical Dribbling

EA's public FIFA 22/23 material describes:
- adjusting stride and running cadence on approach to the ball;
- calculating the path to the ball between touches;
- clean footsteps between dribble contacts;
- interruptible dribble animations for responsive direction changes;
- distinct first-touch / composed-strike setup behavior.

The lesson is not "use ML." The lesson is that **the path and steps between touches are part of ball control**.

Sources:
- `https://www.ea.com/games/fifa/fifa-22/hypermotion`
- `https://www.ea.com/able/news/pitch-notes-fifa-23-gameplay-deep-dive`
- GDC 2022 HyperMotion full-match mocap session.

### FC 26 Dynamic Dribbling

EA's FC 26 gameplay notes specifically call out:
- retuned dribble touch intervals;
- foot-preference logic and animation branching;
- reduced feeling of being locked into animations;
- improved jog-dribble ball-direction logic;
- effort touches on drastic turns.

Source: `https://www.ea.com/games/ea-sports-fc/fc-26/news/pitch-notes-fc26-gameplay-deep-dive`

### FC 27 off-balance dribbling

FC 27 publicly emphasizes maintaining player control while a capable dribbler is visibly off balance under contact, allowing continued directional control rather than simply entering an uncontrollable stumble state.

Source: EA SPORTS FC 27 Gameplay Deep Dive, 2026-07-29.

**SoccerShooter implication:** bad body state should degrade the *quality and options of the next physical contact*, not arbitrarily take input away.

## 3.2 Motion-control research

### DReCon — Bergamin, Clavet, Holden, Forbes (2019)

DReCon uses motion features such as foot positions/velocities, hip velocity, and desired future trajectory. Its trajectory query blends current character motion with desired user motion over a short horizon.

**Use here:** deterministic short-horizon trajectory prediction and foot-phase state, without adopting DReCon's learned full-body physics controller as a browser dependency.

DOI: `10.1145/3355089.3356536`

### Motion Matching at EA / production motion matching

Production motion-matching systems commonly compare pose features and current/future/past trajectory features. SoccerShooter's binding design explicitly says not to build a massive motion-matching system first, so Stage 05R will use the same *feature concepts* inside a deterministic planner/state machine.

## 3.3 Soccer biomechanics

### Zago et al. (2016), Dribbling determinants in sub-elite youth soccer players

Faster dribblers in the study showed about 30% greater foot-ball contact cadence (`3.0 ± 0.1` vs `2.3 ± 0.2 contacts/s`) and more economical/narrow movement paths.

DOI: `10.1080/02640414.2015.1057210`

**Use here:** touch frequency should be coupled to movement/gait and skill/context. These youth values are a calibration reference, not a universal hardcoded law.

### Dos'Santos et al. (2018), angle-velocity trade-off

Faster approaches and sharper changes of direction increase braking/deceleration and re-acceleration demands. A faster approach compromises the ability to execute a sharp direction change cleanly.

DOI: `10.1007/s40279-018-0968-3`

### Singh et al. (2025), biomechanical determinants of change-of-direction performance

Review findings include shorter ground-contact time, braking/propulsive force, trunk inclination, lower center of mass, and joint power as important COD determinants.

DOI: `10.1007/s40279-025-02278-3`

**Use here:** hard cuts must have a measurable plant/braking/propulsion sequence, not merely a different yaw target.

## 3.4 Soccer-specific full-body control research

### Hong et al. (2019), Physics-based full-body soccer motion control for dribbling and shooting

The work tightly couples data-driven motion prediction with physics-based synthesis and explicitly refines motion so the foot reaches the moving ball at the right time and place.

DOI: `10.1145/3306346.3322963`

### PhysicsFC (2025)

PhysicsFC separates Move, Dribble, Trap, and Kick skills and trains transitions between them for a physics-based user-controlled footballer.

DOI: `10.1145/3731425`

**Use here:** keep movement/dribble/trap/kick as explicit states with defined transitions. Do not import a heavyweight learned controller into the production critical path.

# 4. Target architecture — Intent → Steps → Contact

## 4.1 Principle

The user expresses **intent**. The controller predicts a short athletic path and feasible **steps**. Football actions happen only at a legal **contact** generated by those steps.

The player root remains deterministic and authoritative. Animation never gets to teleport the player or ball. The ball remains independent Stage 04 physics.

## 4.2 New authoritative motion state

Extend player state with a compact deterministic `AthleticMotionState`:

```ts
interface AthleticMotionState {
  gait: 'idle' | 'walk' | 'jog' | 'sprint' | 'brake' | 'plant' | 'recover';
  gaitPhase: number;          // 0..1
  supportFoot: 'left' | 'right' | 'both';
  nextFoot: 'left' | 'right';
  strideSeconds: number;
  plantIntensity: number;     // 0..1
  turnDemand: number;         // signed / normalized
  brakingDemand: number;      // 0..1
  propulsionDemand: number;   // 0..1
  bodyLean: { forward: number; lateral: number };
  plannedVelocity: { x: number; z: number };
}
```

Keep it compact and deterministic. Do not add a 60-bone physical character to authoritative state.

## 4.3 Short-horizon movement trajectory

Add a `MovementTrajectoryPlanner` that predicts roughly the next 0.4–0.7 s in a few samples from:
- current velocity;
- user desired direction/magnitude;
- sprint/precision intent;
- acceleration/braking limits;
- requested turn angle;
- current gait/plant state.

Use a critically damped intent transition rather than immediately replacing the future trajectory. Large high-speed turns therefore naturally create brake/plant/re-acceleration demand.

## 4.4 Football contact plan

New shared contract:

```ts
type ContactSurface = 'inside' | 'outside' | 'instep' | 'sole' | 'toe';

interface FootballContactPlan {
  kind: 'dribble' | 'trap' | 'pass' | 'shot';
  foot: 'left' | 'right';
  surface: ContactSurface;
  prepareTick: number;
  contactTick: number;
  recoveryEndTick: number;
  expectedBallPoint: FootballVec3;
  expectedFootPoint: FootballVec3;
  targetBallVelocity: FootballVec3;
  maxLinearImpulse: number;
  maxAngularImpulse: number;
  timingWindowTicks: number;
  geometryTolerance: number;
  quality: number;
  reason: string;
}
```

A planned contact is **permission to attempt**, not guaranteed success. At contact tick the authoritative simulation rechecks geometry, height, ball speed, body state, pressure, and opponent interference.

## 4.5 Contact provenance invariant

Every gameplay ball impulse from a player must be explainable as one of:
- a planned foot/body contact whose contact window and geometry were valid;
- an explicit collision/challenge/interception event.

No routine dribble impulse may exist only because a generic cooldown reached zero.

## 4.6 Lightweight presentation proxy before final art

Add a Stage-05-only articulated QA athlete made from simple shapes:
- pelvis/root;
- left/right upper/lower legs;
- visible feet;
- basic trunk lean;
- procedural gait phase;
- planted-foot lock;
- target contact foot.

This is **not final character art**. Its purpose is to prove the authoritative contact plan can be visibly represented without foot sliding or remote ball impulses. It should later be replaceable by the Stage 12/13 skeleton/animation graph without changing gameplay contracts.

## 4.7 Training-only Contact Ghost

A unique SoccerShooter differentiator: in Training Academy / debug only, optionally show:
- next support foot;
- predicted footfall;
- planned ball-contact point;
- safe/late/overstretched timing band;
- expected outgoing ball vector.

This turns the physical control system into something players can actually learn. Production matches hide it by default. The mechanic remains skill-based rather than opaque.

# 5. Dribbling redesign

Dribbling is the first subsystem migrated because it is the most frequent player-ball interaction and the largest owner complaint.

## 5.1 Replace corridor servo with a contact planner

Create `dribblePlanner.ts`. Every frame it may update the **next** plausible contact plan, but it may only apply a ball impulse at the selected contact window.

Inputs:
- player root position/velocity;
- `AthleticMotionState`;
- predicted movement trajectory;
- ball position/velocity/spin;
- user desired direction;
- precision/sprint intent;
- pressure/opponent proximity;
- preferred foot and current next foot;
- previous touch/contact history.

Outputs:
- no contact planned, or one `FootballContactPlan`;
- chosen foot + surface;
- desired contact time;
- outgoing velocity target;
- setup cost / difficulty / quality.

## 5.2 Foot selection

Use deterministic contextual selection:
- straight carry tends to alternate natural stride contacts;
- modest lateral change prefers the foot/surface that keeps the body between defender and ball where possible;
- inside foot supports controlled inward redirection;
- outside foot supports smaller same-side direction changes and acceleration touches;
- sole becomes available mainly in slow precision/trap contexts;
- preferred foot biases but does not monopolize all contacts;
- weak-foot use can widen quality/error envelope later through player attributes.

Do not fake personality with random foot choices.

## 5.3 Touch cadence

Remove `controlTouchCooldown` as the source of dribble cadence.

Cadence comes from:
- stride duration;
- current foot phase;
- ball intercept time;
- control mode;
- requested turn;
- whether the ball is already too close/far for the next natural step.

Use Zago's approximately 2.3–3.0 contacts/s youth-dribbling result as one diagnostic reference for normal/jog calibration, not a hard global constant. Capture actual Stage-05R cadence histograms and tune by speed/context.

Sprint should naturally have fewer, longer control events because stride and ball travel are longer; close control should create more viable contact opportunities by shortening stride and movement speed.

## 5.4 Ball path between touches

Between contacts the ball is entirely free Stage 04 physics.

The controller predicts where it will be at candidate footfall times and chooses an approach/contact. It may alter the player's *planned path/step* within bounded responsiveness. It must not continuously recenter the ball.

This is the biggest feel change from the current corridor model.

## 5.5 Turns

For a requested direction change:

### Small angle / low speed
- preserve flow;
- choose inside/outside touch based on foot phase;
- modest outgoing-vector redirection;
- minimal braking.

### Medium angle
- shorten stride;
- increase lateral body lean/turn demand;
- contact moves slightly closer;
- exit speed falls enough to preserve credibility.

### Hard angle / high speed
- schedule a brake step;
- lower target COM proxy / increase plant intensity;
- establish plant foot;
- delay the major redirect until a viable contact foot arrives;
- use an effort touch;
- re-accelerate after contact.

A 120°–180° reversal cannot look like a sphere redirect plus a root yaw change. It needs visible loss of speed and at least one credible plant/recovery beat.

## 5.6 Error without slop

Keep deterministic, causally readable error. Do not add ambient random sideways noise.

Error sources:
- contact too early/late within legal window;
- ball too far from ideal foot point;
- excessive incoming ball speed;
- high requested turn at high approach speed;
- weak balance / off-balance collision;
- pressure/contact from defender;
- using non-preferred foot/surface;
- poor plant state.

Error effects:
- more residual incoming momentum;
- slightly wrong contact normal;
- reduced ability to erase lateral velocity;
- heavier or wider touch;
- poorer exit speed.

The player should be able to explain a bad touch after watching it.

## 5.7 Beginner-friendly, high mastery ceiling

### Beginner assistance
- choose a reasonable viable foot/surface automatically;
- generous but physical timing window on unpressured routine touches;
- modest path correction toward the next feasible intercept;
- preserve user-requested direction rather than guessing a different football action;
- never teleport ball/player and never authorize impossible behind-body contact.

### Mastery
- expert setup can preserve more exit speed through sharp changes;
- close-control timing/angle can keep touches closer;
- deliberate sprint knock-ons can exploit space;
- pressure and off-balance situations reward pre-positioning and use of the correct foot;
- future player attributes can narrow/widen contact windows and quality envelopes without changing global physics.

The first minute should work with WASD + sprint + precision. The thousandth hour should reward anticipation, body setup, contact angle, and foot choice.

**Assistance may not change:**
- ball gravity/drag/friction;
- player top speed secretly;
- hard physical reach;
- opponent collision outcome;
- whether a physically impossible contact is legal.

**Calibration matrix:**
- straight walk/jog carry;
- close-control slalom;
- 30/60/90/120/180° changes at multiple approach speeds;
- sprint knock-on then release/cut;
- pressure from left/right;
- weak-foot side;
- loose-ball recovery;
- trap → exit;
- dribble → shot and dribble → pass transitions.

Collect distributions, not only max separation:
- touch cadence;
- foot usage;
- contact timing error;
- ball distance at contact;
- exit speed retained;
- braking time/distance;
- plant duration;
- lost-control rate;
- input-to-visible-response latency.

## Task 11 — Human playtest gate

Automated green is necessary and insufficient.

### Required owner test sequence

1. **Straight jog:** W only for 8–10 s. Ball should feel touched, not pulled.
2. **Gentle slalom:** alternate ~30–45° changes without precision.
3. **Hard cut:** sprint, release sprint, request 90° cut. Expect a readable plant/brake/contact/exit sequence.
4. **Full reversal:** sprint then 180°. Expect real loss of speed and a viable recovery, not an instant pivot.
5. **Precision:** RMB through tight S-turns. Expect compact frequent contacts and slower body movement.
6. **Sprint knock-on:** open space sprint should deliberately push the ball farther ahead with recoverable risk.
7. **Mistake test:** intentionally change direction too late at full speed. Expect a football-shaped heavy/wide touch.
8. **Pressure test:** collide/shoulder near the ball. Direction input should remain active while control quality degrades.
9. **Loose-ball test:** deliberately miss a touch. Ball must stay free until physically recovered.
10. **Dribble → shot:** approach at speed, aim, shoot. Setup/contact should be visible and the ball should leave from the contact event.
11. **Dribble → pass:** same requirement for pass.
12. **Camera:** repeat cuts and strikes while judging whether player, ball, and intended path remain readable.

### Human acceptance

Do not merge until the owner explicitly approves.

Target scorecard:
- movement responsiveness median >= 8/10;
- dribbling/control median >= 8/10;
- ball-contact believability median >= 8/10;
- shooting/contact satisfaction median >= 8/10;
- "feels like the ball is magnetically attached" <= 2/10;
- "I can explain why I lost the ball" >= 8/10.

If owner feedback conflicts with an automated feel proxy, investigate the proxy rather than dismissing the feedback.

# 7. Explicit no-go list

Do **not** fix this stage by:
- increasing control reach until every bad touch is recoverable;
- adding stronger continuous corridor recentering;
- making the ball a child of the player transform;
- teleporting position to a foot/target point;
- adding random sideways noise and calling it realism;
- hiding poor movement under camera shake, VFX, or audio;
- freezing input while a long animation finishes;
- letting presentation animation decide authoritative outcome;
- building a huge motion-matching database before the deterministic contact foundation works;
- making a trained RL full-body controller a production dependency;
- starting final Stage 12 character art before this mechanical gate passes.

# 9. Definition of success

The finished Stage 05R build should let a new player hold W and immediately understand the game, while an experienced player can feel and exploit differences in stride, approach angle, foot choice, touch timing, sprint space, body balance, and pressure.

Most importantly, a slow-motion replay should answer a simple question for every important touch:

> **What did the athlete physically do that caused the ball to move that way?**

If the answer is "the control envelope corrected it," Stage 05R has not succeeded.
